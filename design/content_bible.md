# Market of Ash — Content Bible

## Content direction

**Market of Ash** is a trade-and-travel RPG about making useful promises in a region that has learned not to trust promises. The player is not restoring a kingdom or conquering a frontier. They are operating a small caravan inside a network of settlements that survived a catastrophe by becoming practical, suspicious, and interdependent.

The narrative should make trade feel consequential without turning the game into a morality play. A cheap route is not evil, a profitable sale is not automatically cruel, and faction control is not automatically villainous. The important question is whether the player can see who bears the cost of a choice and whether they are building a network that can recover from the next shortage.

The content system therefore has four jobs. It must give the map a memory, make every settlement more than a price modifier, make crew members alter decisions rather than merely improve statistics, and turn the water crisis into a progression of increasingly difficult but understandable commitments.

> **Content rule:** Every major event must change at least one of the following: route access, available information, cargo or money, faction reputation, crew trust, settlement resilience, or the crisis stage.

## Tone and voice

The tone is warm, dry, and observant rather than grimdark. People are tired, capable, and occasionally funny. Dialogue should be short enough to read during a trading decision. Characters speak in concrete nouns—barrels, chalk, bridges, permits, weights, names—rather than abstract speeches about destiny.

The ashland is not empty. It is a place of patched roofs, reused machinery, improvised signs, old road markers, and people who know exactly how long a barrel will last. The catastrophe is mostly seen through infrastructure and habit: a town counts its cups, a bridge crew saves good bolts, and an official carries three seals because the fourth one was lost in the fire.

The writing should avoid a single “correct” moral voice. The Ash Wardens have real reasons to regulate movement. The Free Caravans have real reasons to resist control. The player should be able to criticize either faction while still relying on people inside it.

## Regional story premise

The five-settlement region sits around a chain of old cisterns and shallow wells. The water network was never fully rebuilt after the ashfall. It functions because caravans move clean water, medicine, scrap, charcoal, and food between settlements that each produce or possess something the others lack.

At the start of the campaign, the player sees only ordinary price differences. Within a few travel cycles, the differences reveal a pattern: water reserves are falling faster than the official reports admit. The Wardens want to close routes and ration access. The Free Caravans want to keep traffic moving, even if that means hoarding, speculation, or dangerous night runs. The player’s caravan becomes important because it can still move between all five settlements.

The crisis is not a timer that simply ends the game. It is a state machine that changes the meaning of ordinary decisions. At crisis stage zero, the player is learning the market. At stage one, water and medicine become visibly scarce. At stage two, faction demands and route conditions change. At stage three, the player must decide whether to spend their accumulated advantage on infrastructure, political control, open access, or private brokerage.

## Campaign structure

The four-chapter structure is designed for a 30–60 minute first playthrough. Each chapter contains a visible regional question, a small number of authored beats, and room for the player’s own trade route to create variation. Narrative should not stop the trade loop for long cutscenes; most content arrives through settlement reports, short conversations, event cards, and changed map labels.

| Chapter | Player experience | Required content beats | Systemic proof |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. The First Ledger** | Learn the five settlements and discover that margin and trust are different resources. | Arrival at Ashgate, first route choice, first crew offer, toll dispute or bridge problem. | The player completes a delivery and sees at least one settlement react. |
| **2. Thin Wells** | Recognize that the market is responding to a regional shortage, not random price noise. | Shortage report, fair or exploitative water sale, faction request, second crew conversation. | Crisis stage one changes water and medicine prices and adds new event choices. |
| **3. The Empty Reservoir** | Commit to a recovery posture while the safest-looking choice becomes politically expensive. | Suspicious escort, last dangerous run, reservoir revelation, infrastructure offer. | Crisis stage two changes route risk, available contracts, and faction demands. |
| **4. What Remains** | Decide what the network should become after the player’s interventions. | Final water contract, crew confrontation, regional verdict, ending report. | The ending summarizes material outcomes and visibly changes settlement conditions. |

### Opening sequence: Ashgate

The player begins at Ashgate with modest money, twelve provisions, empty cargo, and no established crew. The first interface should frame the region as a set of needs rather than a list of prices. An Ashgate clerk offers a small grain delivery to Reedwatch. A Free Caravan broker mentions that cloth is moving through Hollow Market. A Warden notice says the Toll Road is “maintained,” without saying who pays for it.

The opening should not require the player to accept a tutorial contract. A first-time player receives a recommended low-risk route, while a returning player can ignore it. The first route forecast teaches the central tradeoff: Old Road is cheap and exposed, Toll Road is expensive and safer, and Dry Cut preserves money at the cost of provisions and time.

## Locations

The existing five locations should remain mechanically distinct and become narratively legible within the first ten minutes. A player should be able to describe each settlement without looking at the price screen.

| Location | Dramatic identity | What the player wants | What the settlement wants from the player |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Ashgate** | The regulated hub where every movement receives a mark. | Reliable contracts and access to safer roads. | Compliance, visible weights, and proof that the caravan will not create panic. |
| **Brine Cross** | A water market trying to pretend saltwater is a temporary inconvenience. | Cheap water and high-value medicine opportunities. | Clean barrels, fair distribution, and someone willing to expose hoarding. |
| **Cinderford** | A foundry town that can repair the region if it can keep its furnaces lit. | Cheap scrap and charcoal, plus caravan upgrades. | Materials, wages, and a route that does not leave workers stranded. |
| **Hollow Market** | A free bazaar where information is traded as aggressively as goods. | Cloth, medicine, rumors, and flexible contracts. | Risk tolerance, discretion, and a willingness to keep the market open. |
| **Reedwatch** | A frontier settlement where the cost of distant decisions is visible in faces and empty shelves. | Cheap grain and meaningful reputation gains. | Water, medicine, and a reason to believe the network will not abandon it. |

Each location needs one authored visual change for the crisis. Ashgate adds ration chalk to its gate. Brine Cross hangs empty jars as warning bells. Cinderford goes dark in one furnace row. Hollow Market turns water sellers away from the public square. Reedwatch moves its missing-person boards beside the well. These changes should be visible in the settlement panel or travel map, not hidden only in prose.

## Characters

The three crew members represent three ways to understand the caravan. Nara reads movement, Jorun protects continuity, and Tess converts social access into leverage. None is a universal best choice. Their relationship state should be a small, readable value with three descriptions—unsettled, trusted, and committed—rather than a hidden affinity score.

### Nara Vey — the road is a document

Nara is a scout who grew up moving between settlements before the Wardens restored the road markers. She believes information should reduce fear, not become another toll. Her mechanical value is route intelligence, but her narrative value is that she notices who is missing from a report.

Her first scene is an offer at Hollow Market: she gives the player a route note for free, then asks whether it should be sold to a rival caravan or shared with Reedwatch. Later, she identifies the sponsor behind the unmarked escort. Her loyalty rises when the player shares useful information or accepts a visibly risky route to preserve another settlement’s access. It falls when the player sells exclusive route knowledge during a crisis.

Nara’s final question is not “Did we win?” It is “Who can still leave?” This makes her the character most closely connected to the open-routes ending.

### Jorun Pale — the reserve is a promise

Jorun is a quartermaster from Cinderford who treats a ledger as a record of people, not just numbers. He is calm, blunt, and uncomfortable with heroic improvisation that has not been provisioned. His mechanical value is reduced provision waste and better capacity warnings.

His first scene is an audit. He points out that the player’s recommended delivery leaves no reserve for a bridge delay. The player can accept his conservative plan, dismiss him, or hire him with a promise to keep a minimum reserve. His later conflict is the profitable shortage run: he does not forbid it, but asks the player to name what happens if the next caravan cannot buy water.

Jorun’s final question is “What did we keep back for tomorrow?” He is the emotional anchor of the shared-wells ending and the sharpest critic of private brokerage.

### Tess Oryn — every door has a hinge

Tess is a fixer with contacts in both factions. She can make a permit appear, find a buyer, or turn a public accusation into a private negotiation. Her mechanical value is additional negotiation choices and a buffer against reputation loss.

Her first scene occurs at Ashgate when she interrupts an official contract review to point out that the stated toll is not the customary toll. The player can hire her for immediate access, ask her to work for a share of the profit, or refuse because the arrangement sounds too convenient. Her personal arc reveals that she once helped a Warden official conceal a failed reservoir inspection.

Tess’s final question is “Are you building a network, or only making yourself difficult to replace?” She can support any ending, but her dialogue should expose the difference between influence and repair.

## Event writing rules

Events should be authored as compact decision scenes, not random paragraphs. Each event needs a readable trigger, a concrete setup, at least two choices, visible requirements, immediate effects, and a follow-up or changed state. A player should understand the likely category of consequence before selecting a choice, even if the exact number remains uncertain.

The four vertical-slice events are:

| Event | Initial lesson | Escalation | Content consequence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **The Gatekeeper’s Chalk** | A safe route can carry a political cost. | A toll dispute becomes evidence of faction pressure. | Wardens reputation, road access, or a delayed but safer negotiation. |
| **The Span at Cinderford** | Cargo can solve a public problem, but sacrificing cargo changes the contract. | A repaired bridge affects more than the player’s caravan. | Settlement resilience, cargo, time, and the foundry relationship. |
| **The Last Clean Barrel** | A high price does not explain who receives the good. | Water sales change the crisis report and faction response. | Money, reputation, resilience, and the player’s reserve. |
| **Three Riders, No Banner** | Safety without provenance creates future exposure. | An escort reveals a hidden faction strategy. | Route risk, information, reputation, and the final contract. |

The design should support authored variants without multiplying the event count. Variants can alter the setup line, requirements, and one choice label based on current location, crew, crisis stage, or reputation. Keep the consequence categories stable so testing remains straightforward.

## Progression

Progression should make the player capable of taking more interesting risks, not turn the economy into an automatic profit engine. The two main tracks are **Caravan Capability** and **Network Trust**. The first improves the caravan’s ability to carry and interpret risk. The second changes which social and infrastructure choices become available.

The first milestone should unlock a larger hold after a profitable run. The second should unlock a shared ledger after three settlements have been visited. The third should expose the shortage through a visible report. The fourth should prove that recovery is possible after sacrificing cargo, time, or reputation. The final milestone opens the ending decision.

A returning player should be able to reach the ending with different combinations of capability and trust. A player who invests in capacity can run a more commercial caravan. A player who invests in trust can win with less money. Neither route should be strictly superior because the crisis requires both material and social leverage.

## Endings

The endings are regional reports, not cinematic morality judgments. Each report should show a map or settlement summary with three material measures: water stability, route openness, and wealth concentration. The five endings are:

**The Wells Remember Everyone** is the shared infrastructure ending. The player spends capital and relationships to stabilize the water network. The region becomes slower and more accountable. The player earns less immediate profit but creates the strongest recovery state.

**The Wells Belong to Those Who Carry** is the adaptive Commons ending. Official relief failed, but post-failure ordinary charcoal deliveries and direct support let Reedwatch build a legitimate public exchange. The player proves that neglect can create a different institution rather than resetting the original quest.

**Order at the Cistern** is the Warden ending. The player gives the Wardens enough legitimacy to secure reservoirs and roads. Travel becomes safer, but access is regulated. The region is stable, more unequal, and less open to independent traders.

**No Road Owns the Sky** is the Free Caravan ending. The player keeps routes open through independent traffic and shared information. The region remains volatile, but no faction can close every road. The player wins by accepting danger and distributing access.

**The Best Margin** is the private brokerage ending. The player becomes indispensable and resolves the crisis for those who can pay first. This is not a joke ending; it is a viable commercial victory with a visible social cost. Crew dialogue should leave the player unsure whether they built a business or captured a bottleneck.

## Agent implementation guidance

Content definitions should use the stable IDs in `content/content_manifest.json`. UI text should be short and split into title, setup, choice label, requirement, and visible result. Effects should be translated into explicit simulation commands rather than parsed from prose at runtime.

The first content implementation should render one event card, one settlement report, and one ending report using static data. The second slice should connect event requirements to current world state. The third should add content validation and deterministic tests for references, choice counts, trigger stages, and ending reachability. Narrative polish comes after the player can see that a choice changed the world.
