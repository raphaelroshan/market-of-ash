# Market of Ash — Frontier Reference Notes for UX

> These notes extract interaction principles from **Frontier: Elite II** and **Frontier: First Encounters** for a compact caravan-trade RPG. They are not a request to reproduce the source games’ assets, layouts, or mechanical complexity.

## Reference findings

Frontier’s appeal lies in the sense that a player is operating a vehicle inside a consequential wider world, rather than selecting abstract transactions from a menu. The experience joins market assessment, cargo constraints, navigation, travel preparation, risk, arrival, and reinvestment into an understandable loop. Its original interfaces were dense and hardware-bound, but the valuable lesson is **contextual commitment**: the player shifts deliberately from a local market into a navigation and departure state, then sees the journey’s consequences before trading again.

A documented early *First Encounters* trade run pairs a limited starting budget and cargo hold with an explicit stock-market purchase, navigation selection, travel preparation, arrival, and sale. It also highlights changing local demand, the cost of mistakes, and the need to inspect the next context before committing.[1] The original *Frontier: Elite II* is an open-ended trade, combat, and navigation game whose later CD32 version added a navigation computer to make the large simulation more approachable.[2]

## Implications for Market of Ash

| Frontier-derived principle | Market of Ash implementation rule |
| --- | --- |
| The player occupies a place before they plan the next move. | The **Settlement Shop** is the home state after launch and arrival. It focuses on local people, needs, cargo, and services—not a map behind every control. |
| Navigation is a deliberate commitment state. | The **Departure Desk** is a separate screen. It owns the map, destination, route comparison, preparation summary, and final commit action. |
| Cargo capacity and operating costs make planning meaningful. | The shop always exposes money, provisions, hold space, selected load cost, and local reasons. The departure screen turns that load into route-specific expected outcomes. |
| A trip matters because it changes the next decision. | A travel sequence must return an arrival report: cargo preserved/lost, provisions/time used, changed market or relationship, and the next settlement’s visible need. |
| Complexity must be learned through action, not a manual. | First-run guidance uses one visible objective, progressive disclosure, plain-language reasons, reversible planning, and no forced choice. |

## Non-negotiables carried forward

Commerce remains the main verb. Every critical price, route risk, fee, provision cost, delay, and expected outcome must have a legible reason. The player is never forced into a weapons path; political and personal choices deepen a trade plan rather than replace it. The first hour permits recovery from a poor trade, and unexplained bankruptcy or campaign-ending random failure is excluded.
