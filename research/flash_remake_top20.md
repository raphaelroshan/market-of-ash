# Top 20 Commercial Remake Opportunities from the Flash Era

**Prepared by Manus AI — August 2026**

## Executive recommendation

The best opportunity is **Frontier**, followed by **Desktop Tower Defense, Rebuild 2, Raze, and The Visitor**. These five have the most attractive combination of a clearly legible core loop, strong evidence of historical audience interest, meaningful room for modern production value, and a product shape that can work as a paid single-player game.

The most important strategic distinction is between a **remake opportunity** and a **licensed sequel opportunity**. Several historically popular Flash games already have commercial successors: *Warfare 1917/1944* now have the *Warfare Legacy Collection*; *Rebuild* has *Rebuild 3*; *Stick RPG* has *Stick RPG 2*; *The King’s League* has *King’s League II*; *GemCraft* has a 2026 legacy collection; and *Strike Force Heroes* has a commercial Steam release.[10][11][12][13][14][15] Those properties can still be attractive, but only through a new license, sequel, or clearly superior reboot. They should not be treated as unclaimed concepts.

The shortlist below instead asks: **Which Flash-era games have a memorable design hook that could support a premium product today, while still leaving enough room to improve depth, usability, presentation, and retention?**

## A note on TRADESMAN: Deal to Dealer

The user’s benchmark is directionally correct: the original *Frontier* has a much stronger and more interesting systemic foundation than its simple presentation suggests. However, the current evidence does not support calling *TRADESMAN: Deal to Dealer* an outright commercial failure. Its Steam page reports **1,431 all-time reviews marked Mostly Positive**, but **41 recent reviews marked Mixed**; it is single-player, remains in Early Access, and was listed at **$2.49** at retrieval.[9]

That pattern suggests a useful product lesson. The fantasy of travelling, trading, recruiting, fighting, and building a fortune is commercially legible, but a modern version must make the first hour immediately understandable and rewarding. The likely opportunity is not merely “add more systems.” It is to preserve the original’s feeling of freedom while improving onboarding, economic feedback, pacing, encounter variety, visual hierarchy, and the relationship between trading and combat. A premium remake should feel like a coherent adventure rather than a spreadsheet surrounded by loosely connected subsystems.

## Evaluation method

Each candidate received an analyst score out of 100. The score is directional and should be validated with rights conversations, prototype testing, and market research before investment.

| Criterion | Weight | Meaning |
|---|---:|---|
| Core-loop quality | 25% | Does the original have a clear, satisfying loop that remains fun after the novelty wears off? |
| Modernization headroom | 20% | Can production value, UX, content, progression, and systemic depth improve substantially without losing identity? |
| Audience evidence | 20% | Are there meaningful portal counts, ratings, favorites, views, awards, or repeated cross-site signals? |
| Execution risk | 15% | Can a small or mid-sized team make a compelling version without requiring an expensive content pipeline or live-service infrastructure? |
| Monetization fit | 10% | Can the concept support a premium PC/console/mobile product, optional DLC, or non-extractive multiplayer? |
| IP feasibility | 10% | How plausible is licensing or creator participation? This is a risk flag, not legal clearance. |

Portal counts are not directly comparable. Armor Games, Kongregate, and Newgrounds used different counters and distribution models, while some current pages now host HTML5 or emulated versions. For example, Armor Games lists *Frontier* at 4,089,402 plays, *Raze* at 36M, and *Toss the Turtle* at 12,362,040 plays, while Kongregate lists *Raze* at 47.0M, *Desktop Tower Defense* at 5.2M, and *The King’s League: Odyssey* at 5.2M.[1][2][3][4][5] These figures are strong signals of historical reach, not an additive market-size estimate.

## The five strongest opportunities in more detail

### 1. Frontier: the best fit for the user’s thesis

*Frontier* has the clearest opportunity to become a premium game because the underlying fantasy is broader than its 2009 interface suggests. Its original structure already combines travel, trading, faction alignment, combat, progression, and open-ended decision-making. Armor Games describes 50 cities across 10 environments and explicitly frames the game as an economics-focused, non-linear RPG.[1]

A modern version should be positioned as a **trade-and-travel adventure RPG**, not as a pure merchant simulator. The player should begin with a clear personal objective, such as paying off a debt, rescuing a family member, or earning a guild charter. Trading should create route decisions, while combat, factions, crew members, local politics, and weather should create reasons to deviate from the most profitable path. The best structure would be a handcrafted regional campaign supported by systemic replayability, not a huge procedurally generated world with little authored content.

| Frontier remake pillar | Recommended direction |
|---|---|
| Core loop | Buy low, transport risk, sell high, reinvest, recruit, choose alliances, survive incidents. |
| Modern hook | Dynamic regional economy where political actions alter supply, demand, routes, and prices. |
| Combat | Small-scale tactical encounters or fast auto-battles with meaningful preparation; avoid making combat a disconnected minigame. |
| Progression | Ship/cart upgrades, crew relationships, faction reputation, trade specializations, and route mastery. |
| Monetization | Premium $19.99–$24.99 PC/console game, with optional expansion regions; avoid energy timers and aggressive microtransactions. |
| Prototype test | A vertical slice containing five towns, three trade goods, two factions, one crew member, and one branching crisis. |

### 2. Desktop Tower Defense: the safest mid-budget design

*Desktop Tower Defense* is an attractive project because its original rules are readable in seconds but can support hundreds of hours of mastery. Kongregate describes a compact loop of placing towers, stopping creeps, purchasing upgrades, selling towers, and managing paths; it also reports 5.2M plays and a 4.2 rating.[4] A Kongregate comment specifically asked whether someone could write the game in a non-Flash format, which is useful qualitative evidence of unmet preservation demand.[4]

The commercial version should not simply copy the old desktop aesthetic. It should retain the deliberate placement and path-building tension while adding a campaign map, optional modifiers, challenge seeds, accessibility features, and an excellent editor. **Workshop-style user maps** would be especially valuable because they lower the need for a continuous stream of developer-authored levels.

### 3. Rebuild 2: the strongest narrative-systems candidate

*Rebuild 2* is more commercially interesting than a conventional zombie shooter because the emotional payoff comes from people, not only combat. Kongregate describes assigning survivors, reclaiming land, managing supplies, housing, morale, skills, equipment, plotlines, and hidden endings.[6] That creates a natural premium structure: each campaign can generate memorable stories, while named survivors provide the emotional continuity that many strategy games lack.

The original property is already covered by *Rebuild 3*, which the official site says is available on PC, Mac, iOS, and Android and supports Steam Workshop.[12] The correct commercial route would therefore be **Rebuild 4**, a licensed sequel with a new setting and stronger presentation, or a spiritual successor with an original world. A modern version should focus on richer relationships, faction diplomacy, more legible consequence chains, and a campaign director that creates escalating dilemmas rather than simply increasing enemy numbers.

### 4. Raze: the strongest action candidate

*Raze* has unusually strong direct audience evidence: Kongregate lists 47.0M plays and a 4.3 rating, while the page’s developer update mentions a Flash Gaming Summit best-shooter award.[3] The concept is also production-friendly. A polished 2D action shooter can be built around a limited number of reusable arenas, weapons, enemy archetypes, and campaign set pieces rather than a large open world.

The recommended commercial version is **a premium campaign-first shooter with optional co-op or asynchronous challenge layers**. The original identity—aliens, robots, zombies, faction switching, platforming, and weapon progression—should remain visible. The main improvement should be feel: tighter movement, stronger hit feedback, better enemy AI, more distinct weapons, and a campaign where human and enemy routes change how levels are played. Multiplayer should be additive rather than the entire business model, because a small team cannot safely promise a permanent competitive population.

### 5. The Visitor: the strongest streamer-friendly project

*The Visitor* demonstrates how a simple premise can become memorable. Newgrounds reports more than 1.6M views, over 5,000 favorites, a 4.60/5 score, and 245 lists; it also records front-page placement, a Weekly Users’ Choice result, and a third-place monthly finish.[8] The original game’s hook—a small alien parasite consuming living creatures through environmental puzzles—is immediately understandable and visually distinctive.

The remake should be a compact, premium, **episodic horror-puzzle adventure** rather than a long open-world game. Each chapter could introduce a new environment, prey ecosystem, mutation, and puzzle grammar. The product would be highly streamable because the audience can watch players discover increasingly grotesque solutions. The principal risks are tone, repetition, and rights; the design must diversify the puzzles while keeping the predator fantasy central.

## Commercial patterns across the shortlist

The most promising products fall into four business shapes.

| Product shape | Best examples | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| Systemic single-player adventure | Frontier, Rebuild 2, Sonny | Strong replayability, authored stories, and DLC-friendly world expansion. |
| Mastery-driven strategy | Desktop Tower Defense, Age of War, The World’s Hardest Game | Low-to-mid content cost, high replayability, and strong controller/PC fit. |
| Premium action loop | Raze, The Last Stand, Hobo, Gunblood | Easy-to-understand trailers, satisfying moment-to-moment play, and optional challenge modes. |
| Creative or physics sandbox | Line Rider, Bubble Tanks, Crush the Castle, Toss the Turtle | User-generated content or systemic combinations can extend the tail without requiring a full live service. |

The evidence also suggests that **single-player should be the default**. Multiplayer is worth adding when it naturally reinforces the original hook—co-op for *Fireboy & Watergirl*, shared creations for *Line Rider*, or optional co-op/PvE for *Raze*—but a small studio should avoid making server population, esports, or matchmaking the foundation of its financial plan.

## Recommended development order

If the objective is to test the thesis with limited capital, I would begin with **Desktop Tower Defense** or a legally distinct spiritual successor. It has a low art burden, a highly testable loop, and a natural prototype: one map, six towers, three enemy types, one upgrade tree, and one challenge modifier can reveal whether the modernization is working.

If the objective is to pursue the strongest differentiated commercial product, I would pursue **Frontier** first through a rights conversation and build a vertical slice before committing to a full game. The prototype should prove that trading, travel risk, crew progression, factions, and encounters form one coherent loop. If rights are unavailable, the same design thesis can become an original “trade-and-travel fantasy RPG” with new names, setting, art, and mechanics.

The third option is **Raze**, particularly for a team with strong 2D action expertise. It provides the clearest audience signal among the less-served candidates and can be scoped around a polished campaign. *The Visitor* is the best compact narrative bet, while *Rebuild 2* is the best high-depth systems bet if licensing is possible.

## Candidates I would not pursue as a simple remake

I would not recommend a straightforward remake of *Kingdom Rush*, *Bloons TD*, *GemCraft*, *Warfare 1917/1944*, *Fancy Pants*, *Motherload*, *Stick RPG*, *The King’s League*, or *Strike Force Heroes*. These have already been commercially ported, remastered, or continued, often by the original rights holders or established publishers.[10][11][12][13][14][15] They remain valuable as benchmarks, and some could support excellent licensed sequels, but they do not represent the clearest unserved market gap.

I would also avoid building around *Super Mario 63*, *Portal: The Flash Version*, or *Super Smash Flash 2* unless licenses are secured. Their popularity is real, but much of their appeal derives from third-party IP, making them poor foundations for an independent commercial product. A spiritual successor can borrow the relevant design lesson—precision platforming, portal-based puzzles, or crossover-style arena combat—without copying protected characters, names, art, music, or level designs.

## Conclusion

The strongest lesson from the Flash era is not that old games should be made prettier. It is that **small games often contained unusually concentrated design ideas**. The commercial opportunity is to preserve that concentration while adding the quality expected of a paid product: better onboarding, stronger feel, richer consequences, coherent progression, accessible controls, good controller support, and a clear reason to keep playing.

For the specific *Frontier* thesis, my recommendation is: **investigate a licensed Frontier reboot first; prototype a legally distinct trade-and-travel RPG in parallel; use Desktop Tower Defense as the lower-risk alternative; and treat TRADESMAN’s mixed recent reception as evidence to over-invest in clarity and pacing rather than simply adding more systems.**
