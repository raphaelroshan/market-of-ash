# Frontier, Rebuild, and Creative Tower Defense
## Comparative design references and reusable product directions

**Prepared by Manus AI — August 2026**

## Executive view

The three concepts sit in different parts of the strategy market, but they share one valuable characteristic: each can turn a small number of understandable actions into a much larger system of consequences.

**Frontier** is strongest when treated as a trade-and-travel adventure RPG rather than a merchant spreadsheet. The best modern references are *Star Traders: Frontiers*, *Merchant of the Skies*, *Recettear*, *Moonlighter*, and *Curious Expedition 2*. Together they show how crew identity, economic choices, travel risk, shop or company growth, and procedural events can make a journey feel personal.

**Rebuild** is strongest when the city is a social machine. The best references are *Rebuild 3*, *The Last Spell*, *Against the Storm*, *Bad North*, *Dome Keeper*, and *This War of Mine*. They demonstrate different ways to combine settlement growth, scarcity, tactical defense, moral pressure, and run-based progression.

**Tower defense** is no longer limited to placing static towers beside fixed lanes. The most useful modern references—*Orcs Must Die! 3*, *Dungeon Warfare 2*, *Ratropolis*, *Isle of Arrows*, *Rogue Tower*, *Creeper World 4*, *Mindustry*, *Dome Keeper*, *Thronefall*, *There Are No Orcs*, and *Booster Pack Heroes*—change the player’s relationship with space, time, resources, enemies, or control.

My strongest product recommendation is to pursue **Frontier as the primary licensed opportunity**, keep **Rebuild as a licensed sequel or high-quality spiritual successor**, and treat **Desktop Tower Defense as the best lower-risk foundation for a new tower-defense game**. The most promising original hybrid is a game in which the player travels between settlements, trades and recruits, then physically prepares and defends the settlement that is under threat. That would combine the emotional and economic potential of *Frontier* with the community stakes of *Rebuild* and the moment-to-moment clarity of tower defense.

## 1. Frontier: what modern games reveal

The original *Frontier* remains a compelling design seed because Armor Games describes a non-traditional RPG with **50 cities across 10 environments**, while earlier portal evidence records more than four million Armor Games plays.[1] The remake should not attempt to compete directly with full-scale sandboxes such as *Mount & Blade* or *Starfield*. Its opportunity is a highly readable, compact world where every journey creates a meaningful decision.

### Frontier-like reference games

| Reference | What it adds to the Frontier formula | What to borrow | What to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| [*Star Traders: Frontiers*][3] | A captain, customizable crew, careers, factions, political conflict, exploration, and emergent events. Steam lists 3,252 Very Positive English reviews, single-player play, Workshop, and leaderboards at retrieval.[3] | Crew roles, faction reputation, captain identity, and events that turn travel into stories. | Excessive system density, opaque rules, and a UI that asks the player to understand the simulation before enjoying it. |
| [*Merchant of the Skies*][2] | A clear vehicle fantasy: fly a ship, sell goods, gather resources, and build a trade company. It is single-player and listed at $14.99 with 1,438 Very Positive reviews at retrieval.[2] | Physical routes, company progression, regional production chains, and an accessible trading interface. | Making routes too safe or reducing the economy to a sequence of obvious arbitrage clicks. |
| [*Recettear: An Item Shop’s Tale*][4] | A concrete debt clock, shop management, market purchases, pricing, interior design, randomized dungeons, and multiple adventurers. Steam lists 5,523 Overwhelmingly Positive English reviews and a $19.99 price.[4] | Attach economic decisions to an urgent personal goal; connect commerce to an active acquisition activity. | A shop loop without enough narrative or strategic variation. |
| [*Moonlighter*][5] | Alternates dungeon expeditions with shopkeeping. Steam describes the player as an adventurous shopkeeper and lists 7,253 Very Positive English reviews.[5] | A satisfying “go out, acquire, return, monetize, upgrade” rhythm. | Treating the two halves as separate games that do not affect one another. |
| [*Curious Expedition 2*][6] | Procedural expeditions, party composition, supplies, decisions, dynamic narration, and authored event fragments. Steam lists 586 Very Positive English reviews.[6] | Short travel legs, crew-specific event outcomes, route risk, and replayable narrative combinations. | Randomness that invalidates careful planning or overwhelms the main economic fantasy. |
| *Sunless Sea* | A darker model of travel risk, fuel, supplies, crew, ports, and story discovery. | Make distance, fuel, cargo, and crew loss create tension. | Excessive punishment that turns discovery into attrition. |

### Recommended Frontier structure

The commercial version should use a **regional campaign** rather than an enormous procedurally generated continent. A first release could contain five regions, roughly 25–35 meaningful towns, 8–12 trade goods, 6–8 factions, and a roster of named crew members. The original “50 cities” idea is valuable as a promise of variety, but the player does not need 50 equally deep cities on day one.

The core loop should be: **read the market, choose a route, prepare the crew and cargo, travel through risk, react to incidents, arrive, trade or fight, and decide what the journey changes**. A town should not simply be a shop with a different price table. It should have a local problem: a harvest failure, a faction embargo, a monster infestation, a strike, a border closure, or a shortage created by the player’s previous actions.

| System | Recommended implementation |
|---|---|
| Economy | Local supply and demand with a small number of visible causes. The player should understand why prices changed. |
| Travel | Routes with forecasted risks, travel time, cargo capacity, fuel or provisions, and opportunities to detour. |
| Crew | Named companions with jobs, traits, relationships, fears, and event-specific abilities. |
| Factions | Reputation that changes permits, prices, escorts, safe passage, contracts, and enemies. |
| Combat | Brief tactical encounters or high-quality preparation-driven battles. Combat should be a consequence of trade and travel, not a disconnected arcade mode. |
| Story | Three or four authored regional arcs supported by systemic events. The player should be able to finish the game through different economic and political identities. |
| Progression | Vehicle upgrades, cargo tools, crew development, trade licenses, faction privileges, and permanent route knowledge. |
| Presentation | Strong map readability, excellent price explanations, and a first-hour tutorial built around one profitable and one dangerous journey. |

The key design principle is **trade should create leverage**. Buying grain cheaply should allow a settlement to survive a siege, win a faction’s trust, or undercut a rival. Smuggling a restricted item should be profitable but may close a route. A crew member may unlock a route but demand a share of the profits. These consequences are what transform merchant actions into RPG decisions.

## 2. Rebuild: what modern games reveal

The original *Rebuild 2* combines survivor recruitment, supplies, housing, morale, skills, equipment, city reclamation, plotlines, and hidden endings.[7] The official *Rebuild* site confirms that Sarah Northway’s *Rebuild 3: Gangs of Deadsville* is already available on PC, Mac, iOS, and Android, with Steam Workshop support.[7] The opportunity is therefore not a basic remake. It is a **new sequel, a major reimagining, or a spiritual successor**.

### Rebuild-like reference games

| Reference | What it adds to Rebuild | What to borrow | What to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| [*Rebuild 3: Gangs of Deadsville*][7] | The direct commercial continuation: city building, survivors, factions, and Workshop support. | Treat survivors as people with histories and allow the campaign to continue after the first successful city. | Merely adding more buildings and zombies without changing the emotional or strategic structure. |
| [*The Last Spell*][8] | Daytime rebuilding and nighttime tactical defense in a roguelite campaign. Steam lists 6,674 Very Positive English reviews, a $24.99 regular price, and several expansions.[8] | A strong management-to-defense cadence, build choices that matter immediately, and a clear end-of-night payoff. | Making every survivor a combat unit and losing the quieter social dimension of Rebuild. |
| [*Against the Storm*][9] | Multiple short settlements, species needs, production chains, hostile conditions, and meta-progression between expeditions. Steam lists 18,958 Overwhelmingly Positive English reviews.[9] | Run-based settlement structure, persistent unlocks, and maps that create different strategic constraints. | Copying its city-builder scale; Rebuild should remain character-led and more intimate. |
| [*Bad North: Jotunn Edition*][10] | Small procedurally shaped islands, unit positioning, evacuation pressure, and tactical defense. Steam lists 5,600 Very Positive English reviews.[10] | Compact maps where terrain creates the challenge and each location feels distinct. | A combat system with no social consequences outside the battle. |
| [*Dome Keeper*][11] | Alternating mining and defense, upgrade choices, return-to-base time pressure, and roguelike runs. Steam lists 11,609 Very Positive English reviews and broad co-op/PvP support.[11] | A second activity that creates urgency before each defense phase. | Adding a second activity merely for variety; it should directly affect survival and settlement growth. |
| *This War of Mine* | Survivor needs, moral dilemmas, scarcity, and personal consequences. | Difficult choices in which survival has a human cost. | Overusing despair or making every decision feel like a punishment. |
| *Frostpunk* | Lawmaking, social order, crisis management, and a settlement-wide moral identity. | Let the player’s policy choices change the city’s culture and future. | A city scale so large that individual survivors become invisible. |

### Recommended Rebuild structure

A stronger modern version would operate on three linked layers. The **district layer** determines what can be reclaimed, fortified, scavenged, or sacrificed. The **survivor layer** tracks named people, relationships, abilities, injuries, beliefs, and rivalries. The **campaign layer** presents a moving regional crisis: another settlement, a hostile faction, a disease, a changing horde, a migration wave, or the loss of a key resource.

The most important modernization is to make the city’s social life more legible. When a survivor dies, defects, becomes a leader, or starts a relationship, the player should understand why that happened and what it changes. A survivor’s skill should be useful, but the survivor’s personality should be what the player remembers.

A commercially attractive structure would be **one campaign made of six to eight districts**, each playable in roughly 30–60 minutes, with a persistent map of relationships and factions. A district could be won by securing its hospital, rescuing civilians, defeating a faction, repairing a power plant, or negotiating a treaty. This creates more variety than simply reclaiming every square on the map.

A particularly strong sequel concept would be **Rebuild after the first winter**, when the problem is no longer only zombies. The settlement must decide whether to remain isolated, join a regional network, trade with rival cities, accept refugees, or become a militarized power. This connects Rebuild naturally to Frontier: resources and trade routes become strategic, while survivors make the consequences personal.

## 3. Creative tower-defense references

The user’s current examples are useful because they each change the conventional formula in a different way. *Sir, We Have an Orc Problem* focuses on overwhelming firepower and incremental escalation; Steam lists 2,244 Very Positive English reviews, a $9.99 price, and a July 28, 2026 release.[12] *There Are No Orcs* shifts the player toward spatial production, auto-battling armies, faction asymmetry, and commander abilities; Steam lists 1,174 Very Positive reviews and a $8.99 price.[13] *Booster Pack Heroes* uses pack-opening, hero collection, spells, upgrades, and chapter progression; Steam listed it as coming soon with no reviews at retrieval.[14]

These are not just cosmetic variations. They change what the player is optimizing.

| Reference | Core twist | Strategic question it creates |
|---|---|---|
| [*Orcs Must Die! 3*][18] | Third-person combat plus trap placement, large War Scenarios, War Machines, Endless, Weekly Challenges, and evolving Scramble modifiers. Steam lists 4,946 Very Positive English reviews.[18] | What can I build, and where must I personally intervene? |
| [*Dungeon Warfare 2*][19] | Physics traps, environmental hazards, 33 traps with eight traits each, and enemies with special traits. | How can I manipulate space and momentum rather than simply increase damage? |
| [*Ratropolis*][15] | Real-time deckbuilding, city building, roguelite progression, and tower defense. Steam lists 1,711 Very Positive English reviews and a $17.99 price.[15] | Which cards and production choices create a stable defense economy this run? |
| [*Isle of Arrows*][16] | Randomly drawn tiles build the fortress and its paths; campaigns unlock new elements. Steam lists 935 Very Positive reviews and a $12.99 price.[16] | Where should the next piece of the board go, and how much space should I reserve? |
| [*Rogue Tower*][17] | The path continuously expands and the player influences its shape; tower upgrades arrive through card draws. Steam lists 4,366 Very Positive English reviews.[17] | How can I make the enemy’s route longer, more concentrated, or more vulnerable? |
| [*Creeper World 4*][20] | The enemy is a physics-based fluid that floods terrain; the game includes a mission generator, editor, custom units, and custom modes. | How do I shape territory and infrastructure against a moving spatial process? |
| [*Mindustry*][21] | Factory automation feeds ammunition and materials to defenses while the player also attacks enemy bases. Steam lists 9,727 Very Positive English reviews.[21] | How do I make my logistics network strong enough to sustain both defense and offense? |
| [*Dome Keeper*][11] | Mining and defense alternate under time pressure. | How much can I safely explore before I must return to defend? |
| [*Thronefall*][22] | Minimalist kingdom construction by day and defense by night. Steam lists 10,985 Overwhelmingly Positive English reviews.[22] | Which limited buildings and units best prepare this particular night? |
| [*Bad North*][10] | Small island geometry, unit positioning, evacuation, and roguelite progression. | Which units can cover this terrain before the next landing? |
| *There Are No Orcs* [13] | Tile-building, auto-battling troops, three factions, and 30 commanders with exclusive buildings. | How can I assemble a spatial economy whose army wins without constant micromanagement? |
| *Booster Pack Heroes* [14] | Pack reveals, rarity, heroes, spells, incremental power, and chapters. | How should I convert a limited collection into a build that survives the next chapter? |

## 4. The most reusable tower-defense innovations

### A. Make the path a resource

Traditional tower defense gives the player a map and asks where to place towers. *Isle of Arrows* and *Rogue Tower* make the path itself something the player constructs or influences.[16] [17] This is one of the highest-value innovations because it turns every building choice into both an economic and defensive decision.

A new version of *Desktop Tower Defense* could use a **growing desktop or city grid**. The player places roads, buildings, towers, and resource nodes. The path is not fully fixed; it is shaped by what the player builds. The trade-off is that a short path may be cheap and efficient early but becomes impossible to defend later.

### B. Make the enemy a process, not a unit list

*Creeper World 4* makes the enemy a fluid that floods terrain.[20] This is a powerful way to create a different genre identity. Other possibilities include smoke, fire, disease, noise, corruption, or political influence. Towers then solve spatial problems rather than only targeting individual enemies.

For a *Rebuild*-adjacent game, the enemy could be **an outbreak that spreads through population density and infrastructure**. The player would not only kill enemies; they would manage quarantines, bridges, hospitals, supply lines, and evacuation routes.

### C. Add a second activity that matters

*Dome Keeper* alternates mining and defense, while *Moonlighter* alternates adventure and commerce.[5] [11] The lesson is not simply to insert a minigame. The second activity should produce the resources, information, or relationships that change the defense phase.

A new tower-defense game could alternate between **expedition and fortification**. During the expedition, the player scavenges materials, rescues specialists, discovers enemy weaknesses, and chooses which route to secure. During the defense, those choices become towers, traps, units, or special abilities.

### D. Make the economy part of combat

*Mindustry* turns ammunition and production logistics into the core challenge.[21] *Ratropolis* makes cards and city production interact with defense.[15] *There Are No Orcs* ties building placement to army production.[13] The best version of this idea is not a second spreadsheet; it is a direct cause-and-effect chain that the player can see.

For example, an iron mine could feed cannon ammunition, but also attract raiders. A grain farm could increase settlement morale, but take up a strong tower position. A trade route could import medicine, but create a predictable enemy approach. This is exactly where a Frontier/Rebuild/tower-defense hybrid becomes interesting.

### E. Give the player an active commander

*Orcs Must Die! 3* proves that direct control can make defense feel physical and dramatic, while *Bad North* proves that small-unit positioning can create tactical depth without a huge battlefield.[10] [18] The player does not need a fully featured action RPG. A single controllable captain, engineer, scout, or commander can create the feeling that the player is participating rather than only observing.

The recommended approach for a mid-sized project is **light direct control**: move a commander, deploy temporary abilities, repair structures, rescue units, or redirect enemy attention. Avoid building an expensive hero roster unless it is central to the game’s identity.

### F. Use cards and randomness to create decisions, not chores

*Rogue Tower*, *Ratropolis*, *Isle of Arrows*, and *Booster Pack Heroes* show several ways to use randomness for replayability.[13] [14] [15] [16] [17] The best rule is that the player should normally receive several viable options rather than a single correct answer.

For a premium game, I would avoid monetized randomized packs. *Booster Pack Heroes* can be studied for its reveal rhythm, but a paid product should use **earned, deterministic, or openly disclosed collection systems** rather than monetized scarcity. Cards should be a design language for choices, not a reason to pressure the player into purchases.

## 6. Recommended priority by project

### Frontier

The preferred direction is **a compact regional trade-and-travel RPG with named crew, faction politics, dynamic local crises, and light tactical encounters**. The first prototype should contain five towns, three factions, four trade goods, three crew members, two route hazards, and one crisis that changes prices and faction relations. The key test is whether players understand the market and feel that their first profitable journey created new opportunities.

The main risk is feature sprawl. *Star Traders: Frontiers* demonstrates how much depth the format can support, but it is also a warning about complexity.[3] The project should prioritize the feeling of “I chose this route and now the world remembers it” over the number of commodities or ship parts.

### Rebuild

The preferred direction is **a district-based survivor strategy game with day planning, named relationships, and night defense**. It should be developed only as a licensed sequel or a clearly original spiritual successor because *Rebuild 3* already exists as the official continuation.[7]

The first prototype should contain one district, eight survivors, four buildings, three threat types, one faction, and one moral crisis. The test is whether players care about at least three individual survivors and can explain why their district succeeded or failed.

### Desktop Tower Defense and creative tower defense

The preferred direction is **a tower-defense game in which the player constructs the battlefield and the economy simultaneously**. The strongest first prototype would combine three ideas: player-shaped paths from *Isle of Arrows* and *Rogue Tower*, a small production economy from *There Are No Orcs* and *Ratropolis*, and a direct commander or emergency ability from *Orcs Must Die! 3*.[13] [15] [16] [17] [18]

The prototype should avoid trying to be *Mindustry*, *Orcs Must Die!*, and *Against the Storm* at once. Choose one sentence that explains the game. My preferred sentence is: **“Build the town that produces your defenses, shape the road the enemy must take, and personally save the settlement when your plan starts to fail.”**

## Conclusion

The modern references point toward a common opportunity: **make the player’s preparation visible in the crisis**. In Frontier, the route, cargo, crew, and political choices should determine what happens at the next town. In Rebuild, relationships, food, morale, and construction should determine who can hold the district. In tower defense, the player should shape the path, economy, enemy process, or commander role instead of only choosing which tower has the highest damage.

The most attractive overall concept is therefore not a generic “modernized Flash game.” It is a focused systemic game with a strong fantasy, short readable decisions, and consequences that carry forward. If licensing is possible, start with **Frontier**. If licensing is not possible, build a spiritual successor that combines a travelling merchant, a fragile community, and defenses shaped by trade. For a lower-risk production path, build the **living-maze/Desktop Tower Defense** concept first and use its prototype to test the underlying thesis.
